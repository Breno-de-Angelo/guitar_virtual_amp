\section{Comparison, Failure Modes and Open Problems}
\label{sec:comparison}

\subsection{Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:methods} places the main methods by what they need and what
they return. Three properties are required at once by the commercial case:
no dry signal of the target, an output that a real-time chain can use, and
robustness to separated stems. Effect recognition (Section~\ref{sec:recognition})
returns usable parameters but has been trained and tested almost only on
synthetic, isolated renders. Unpaired modelling (Section~\ref{sec:nodi}) needs
no aligned dry signal and returns a real-time model, but has covered only
distortion on isolated guitar. Tone transfer (EG-VAE) handles whole chains,
including drive, amplifier, cabinet, modulation, delay and reverb, but returns
audio. Inference-time search (ST-ITO) drives any chain but fails on guitar
tone with an embedding objective. StemFX is the only work trained on separated
stems, for mixing effects. No method in the table has all three properties.

\subsection{Known and expected failure modes}

\emph{Separation artefacts.} Effect retrieval roughly halves from isolated to
separated stems~\cite{yeh2025fxencoder}. Reverberation tails, stereo delays and
wide choruses are the parts most exposed to leakage and masking. Bleed from
keyboards or other guitars adds energy that a matcher will try to reproduce
(expected).

\emph{Several guitars in one stem.} Double-tracked, layered or overdubbed
guitars give one stem with mixed tones. Pan splitting separates parts on
different sides, not parts in the same position (expected for any method).

\emph{Mixing and mastering.} Bus compression, limiting and mix equalisation
act on the guitar after the guitarist's chain. An estimator will attribute
them to the guitar unless it models them; a final linear match absorbs the
equalisation but not the dynamics (expected).

\emph{Non-uniqueness.} Gain staging between a drive pedal and the amplifier,
fuzz against high-gain amplification, compression against picking dynamics,
and the order of time-based effects can produce near-identical sounds. Device
identity is confused even between clean renders of similar
circuits~\cite{comunita2021guitar}, and order is harder than presence~\cite{wada2025hyperbolic}.
For reproduction this is harmless as long as the result sounds alike, which is
why audio-domain objectives are preferred to parameter
losses~\cite{peladeau2024blind}.

\emph{Equipment outside the chain.} The guitar, its pickups and the
microphone placement shape the tone and are not part of an effects chain;
equalisation can only compensate. A mono chain cannot reproduce stereo
effects. A chain with a few generic blocks may lack the character of a
specific amplifier, as EG-VAE's differentiable baseline
showed~\cite{yeh2026egvae}.

\emph{Search pathologies.} LFO rates and delay times make rugged
landscapes~\cite{okita2026audio,uzrad2024diffmoog}; an embedding objective can
improve while the sound moves away~\cite{long2026tape}; errors of a removal
model add to errors of the estimate~\cite{take2024audio}.

\emph{Tone changes within a song.} Rhythm and lead sections, clean verses and
distorted choruses need different presets. Systems that segment songs into
tone sections exist only in products~\cite{bouharb2026gptonebuilder}.

\subsection{Open problems}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Estimating an interpretable amplifier, cabinet and effects chain from a
separated stem of a commercial song, evaluated against known equipment.
\item A benchmark for that task: commercial-like mixes with known chains, and
real multitracks with the guitarist's DI and amplifier tracks, scored with
metrics validated by listening tests.
\item Unpaired or blind modelling of a full chain, not only of a distortion,
and on separated rather than isolated guitar.
\item Delay, reverberation and modulation estimation on real guitar phrases,
and metrics validated for them.
\item Training data that can be shipped: most realistic sets and the largest
capture libraries are non-commercial or forbid bulk use (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}).
\end{enumerate}
